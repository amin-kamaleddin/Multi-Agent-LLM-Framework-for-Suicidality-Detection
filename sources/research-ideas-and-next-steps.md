We are close to publication quality work, but right now its main contribution still reads as a broad model comparison rather than a clear research advance. The fastest way to make it feel more novel is to move the headline away from “which LLM is best” and toward “what kind of risk formulation actually works.” Our results already show that the core scientific issue is not model ranking but label ambiguity, especially the repeated confusion between Indicator and Ideation, and the weak performance on Behavior. That should become the central claim of the paper, because it is the most interesting finding in the results.

The first change I would make is to simplify or restructure the label space. At the moment, the work shows that the current four-way taxonomy is not cleanly separable for zero-shot LLMs, with Ideation performing best and Behavior consistently lagging, while Indicator and Ideation are often confused. Rather than presenting that as a weakness of the models alone, present it as evidence that the task definition itself is unstable in its current flat form. A much stronger version for submission would be a hierarchical setup: first self-risk versus no self-risk, then ideation versus action, then attempt versus non-attempt. That makes the work more significant because you are no longer only benchmarking models; you are proposing a clinically more usable formulation of the task. **This can be done using a multi-agentic system, each agent being responsible for one paradigm of suicidality.**

The second essential change is to add a true zero-risk or neutral class. You already identify this as a limitation, and without it the task is artificially easier and less realistic because every input is guaranteed to belong to some suicide-related category. For a screening paper, that is a serious gap. Even a modest hard-negative set would help a lot: ordinary distress, depression without suicidality, unrelated personal posts, and general discussion about suicide. This one addition would immediately make the study more practically relevant and strengthen the claim that this framework can support real screening rather than closed-set label assignment. I also do not understand the naming convention here; why EvalRing? We definitely need to change this ...

The synthetic-data contribution also needs to be tightened. At the moment, the synthetic dataset is interesting, but the paper does not yet show that it materially helps beyond being easier for the same model to classify. **Since gpt-5-mini both generated and evaluated that dataset, the current result risks reading as a same-model distribution advantage rather than evidence of true usefulness**. The minimum change I would recommend is a train-on-synthetic, test-on-real analysis, or at least cross-model validation where one model generates and different models evaluate. Even better would be a small blinded human review of synthetic samples for realism and label fidelity \[that can take time but highly valuable \- Amin can take a lead on that\!\]. Without that, the synthetic section feels promising but not yet decisive.

You should also sharpen the evaluation target. Macro F1 is reasonable, but for this application the paper needs one risk-sensitive analysis that reflects real use. Since our results show systematic under-classification of severe cases, add a clinically oriented view such as recall on high-risk classes, or collapse Behavior plus Attempt into an “urgent action” group and report performance there. That would make the work more significant because it speaks directly to what matters in deployment: missing high-risk cases is worse than confusing two lower-severity labels. Right now the paper hints at that issue, but it does not fully operationalize it.

A short but important addition would be uncertainty handling. **The current paper argues for screening, but the system always predicts a class. A better framing is selective screening: predict only when confident, otherwise abstain and escalate.** That is both more novel and more defensible in a mental-health setting. You do not need a large new experiment for this. A thresholded confidence or disagreement-based abstention analysis on the existing outputs would already strengthen the paper and make it look more practically mature.

On the code side, the framework is useful, but the paper currently oversells novelty at the framework level. The framework is cleaner if you present it as a reproducible experimentation scaffold, not as a major methodological contribution on its own. The codebase does support multiple prompting modes, retries, caching, and run recovery, which is good, but the strongest code-related improvement before submission would be to add one or two reliability checks that directly support the paper’s claims. In particular, you need better tests around label normalization and output parsing, because this project is very sensitive to formatting variation and class-name consistency. The repository currently has essentially no real test suite, which makes the framework feel less mature than the paper suggests.

There is also a code-level issue in the current design of the synthetic-data pipeline: it rewrites posts directly toward a target label, which makes it likely that generated samples become cleaner and more definition-conforming than real data. That is probably why the synthetic set looks easier. To make that part more credible, generation should preserve ambiguity rather than remove it. In practice, that means prompting for subtle rewrites, controlling lexical overlap with the source, and rejecting samples that become too explicit or templatic. A lightweight adversarial filter or similarity filter would already help.

To sum up \-\> For the submission version, I would not try to add many more models or modes. That will not materially improve the paper. The absolute needs are to reformulate the task more clearly, add hard negatives, make the synthetic-data claim causal rather than descriptive, and include one clinically meaningful risk analysis. Those changes would make the work feel less like an exploratory benchmark and more like a paper with a clear substantive contribution.

The extra analysis that is most worth doing now is statistical rather than expansive. Add confidence intervals or bootstrap intervals for the main metrics (you do not need to regenerate results, just resample with replacement to report 95% confidence intervals), and test whether the top model differences are actually meaningful …
